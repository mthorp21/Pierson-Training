Fix it fast

The common mistakes, and how the pros fix them.

Every player has a habit holding them back. Find yours, fix it, and drill it until it’s gone.

Skying shots over the bar
Why it happens

Leaning back, planting the standing foot behind the ball, or striking underneath it. All three send it into orbit.

The fix

Plant your standing foot right beside the ball, toes pointing at the target. Get your knee and chest over the ball. Point your toe down, lock your ankle, and strike the middle of the ball with your laces. Land on your shooting foot and follow through low toward the goal.

Drill it

Ten shots from 12 yards. Only count it if it goes in below knee height. Hard and flat becomes the habit.

Cue: “Knee over, land on it.”
Shots with no power
Why it happens

Hitting with the toe or the inside of the foot, or swinging only from the knee.

The fix

Approach at a slight angle, make your last stride a little longer, and swing from the hip. Laces through the middle of the ball, body moving forward through the strike.

Drill it

Five shots focusing only on the long last step. Power comes from the approach, not from swinging harder.

Cue: “Long last step.”
First touch bouncing away
Why it happens

The foot is stiff when the ball arrives, so it bounces off like a wall.

The fix

Relax your ankle and pull your foot back slightly as the ball hits it, like catching an egg. Push the touch into space away from pressure, not dead under you.

Drill it

Wall passes for three minutes. Every touch has to stop within one step and set you up for the next pass.

Cue: “Soft and away.”
Passes that roll short or wide
Why it happens

Open, floppy ankle, or looking at the target instead of the ball at the moment of contact.

The fix

Standing foot points at the target. Lock the ankle, toe up, and strike the middle of the ball with the inside of your foot. Follow through straight at your target.

Drill it

Two cones as a gate five yards away. Ten passes through it with each foot.

Cue: “Point, lock, follow.”
Losing it while dribbling
Why it happens

Big touches in traffic and head down, so defenders read you early.

The fix

Small touches, nearly every step, when defenders are close. Save bigger touches for open space. Get your eyes up between touches.

Drill it

Cone weave with a call out: someone holds up fingers, you shout the number while dribbling.

Cue: “Small in traffic, big in space.”
Caught watching the ball
Why it happens

Locked on the ball and not knowing where the pressure is when it arrives.

The fix

Check over both shoulders before the ball comes to you. Know your next move before your first touch.

Drill it

Wall passing with a rule: one shoulder check before every single receive.

Cue: “Look, then receive.”
Crosses and long balls ballooning
Why it happens

Leaning back and hitting under the ball when you meant to drive it.

The fix

To drive it flat, stay over the ball and strike through the middle, the same as shooting. Only lean back and get under the ball when you actually want height.

Drill it

Ten long passes to a partner or target. Keep them below head height.

Cue: “Over it to drive it.”

Game face

The rules coaches notice from across the field.

Coaches can see your body language before they ever see your skills. Make it say “pick me.”

1

No negative face.

Coaches watch your face after a mistake more than the mistake itself. Shake it off and get back in it.

2

Chest up. Always.

Head down tells a coach you’re done. Chest up tells him you want the next ball.

3

Jog everywhere.

Never walk between drills or to a line. Hustle is the easiest thing to stand out with.

4

Hands off your hips.

Hands on hips says you’re tired. Hands on knees says it louder. Stand tall.

5

Pick people up.

Teammate messes up? “Next one.” “You’re good.” Coaches are building a team, not picking solo players.

6

Clap for good plays.

Even when the other guy makes it. Coaches notice who respects the game.

7

Talk loud.

“Man on.” “Turn.” “Good ball.” Quiet players disappear on the field.

8

No eye rolls. No excuses.

Not at the ref, the coach, or a teammate. Ever.

Remember

You control all eight. No talent required.

A word from the legends

How the best handled the hard parts.

Every legend has missed, been cut, or been told no. What made them legends was the next touch.

Kylian Mbappé, age 11

At 11, Mbappé played a youth tournament semifinal in a real stadium. He was so scared he barely touched the ball. After the game his mom marched onto the field and told him he could miss 60 chances and nobody would care, but playing scared was the thing he’d regret. He says he’s never been afraid on a pitch since.

Miss all you want. Just never play scared.
Lionel Messi
“It took me 17 years and 114 days to become an overnight success.”

He was tiny as a kid, and a big club back home in Argentina passed on him. At 13 he moved across the world to join Barcelona’s academy. He kept showing up early and staying late.

Nobody sees the reps. Do them anyway.
Bukayo Saka

At 19, he missed the deciding penalty in the Euro 2020 final at Wembley, in front of the whole country. Three years later, he stepped up again in a Euro 2024 shootout and buried it.

Your worst moment is never your last moment.
Harry Kane

Arsenal released him from their academy at 8. Later he was loaned out to four smaller clubs before he finally broke through at Tottenham. Today he’s England’s all time top scorer.

Getting cut isn’t the end. It’s fuel.
Jamie Vardy

Released at 16 for being too small. He worked in a factory while playing non league on weekends. At 29 he won the Premier League with Leicester and scored in 11 straight games, a league record.

It’s never too late to prove them wrong.
Luka Modrić

He grew up during a war in Croatia, kicking a ball around a hotel parking lot. A big club told him he was too small and weak. He went on to win the Ballon d’Or and multiple Champions Leagues with Real Madrid.

Size doesn’t decide it. Work does.
Cristiano Ronaldo

At 12 he left his family on the island of Madeira to join Sporting’s academy in Lisbon. He was homesick and got teased for his accent. He answered with extra training, every single day.

Discipline beats doubt.
Michael Jordan

As a sophomore, the greatest basketball player ever didn’t make his high school varsity team. He used it as fuel and came back better.

One tryout doesn’t decide who you become.
Michael Jordan
“And that is why I succeed.”

Jordan said he’d missed more than 9,000 shots, lost almost 300 games, and missed 26 game winners when his team trusted him to take the shot.

Every miss is a rep.
Michael Jordan
“I can accept failure, everyone fails at something. But I can’t accept not trying.”

From his book on chasing excellence.

Try everything. Hold nothing back.
Steph Curry
“Success is not an accident.”

Too small, too skinny. The big college programs passed on him, so he went to Davidson and outworked everyone. Now he’s the greatest shooter of all time. In his book he writes that the work you put in before the moment is what carries you through it.

The work is already in your legs. Trust it.

Mistakes are normal. Getting right back into it is the skill.

Workouts

Strength, speed, and staying healthy.

Strong players win more battles, sprint faster, and stay healthy all season.

The rules of lifting at 14

Form first, weight second. Only add weight when every rep of every set looks clean.

Lift with a coach, trainer, or parent watching. Never max out.

Two strength days a week is plenty. Never the day before a game.

Growing fast? Sore knees or heels are common at your age. Tell your parents or coach instead of pushing through.

Cue: “Clean reps only.”

Color for recovery: fruits and veggies at every meal.

Mental game

Stay in the next action.

The best players aren’t the ones who never make mistakes. They’re the ones whose next action is always good.

Your routine before kickoff

Same every time: warm up, picture your first touch, say your focus word, first touch of the ball. A routine tells your body it has done this before.

Cue: “Routine, then play.”

Control what is yours

Effort, voice, body language, and how fast you react. Nothing else needs your attention.

Cue: “My effort, my voice.”

Next action

A mistake is finished the moment it happens. The only thing coaches remember is what you do in the next five seconds.

Cue: “Next action.”

The three second reset

One breath out, tap your shin guard, say your word, find the ball. Practice it tonight after a missed touch so it’s automatic tomorrow.

Body language speaks first

Chest up, eyes forward, jog to every line. Coaches read posture before they read skill. See Game face for the eight rules.

Pressure is a privilege

The feeling in your stomach means this matters. Breathe into it and use it as energy.

Focus

Calm body, clear eyes, loud voice.

The best players have already played the game in their heads before they step on the pitch.

Your highlight reel

Every pro does this. Close your eyes and watch yourself play tomorrow before it happens. Tap through the clips tonight in bed and again in the car.

The Mbappé method

Before big games, Mbappé studies the defenders he’ll face: how they move, their habits, where space will open. He wants to arrive already knowing the game.

Then he keeps it light. He laughs with teammates in the locker room and says “The match begins when the referee blows the whistle, not before.”

Your version: know the plan, then relax until 2:00. The pressure starts at the whistle.

Cue: “Prepared, then free.”

Scan, then decide

Look before the ball comes, so when it arrives the decision is already made. Elite forwards check their shoulder constantly. It’s why they always seem a step ahead.

One word

When the game speeds up, return to one word. “Simple.” “Compete.” “Next.” Choose yours tonight and use it all day tomorrow.

Win the next five minutes

Don’t think about the whole tryout. This drill, this rep, this ball. Then the next one.

Reset button

The same trick penalty takers use when the stadium is loud. Sixteen seconds per round. Use it if the nerves spike before check in.

Tryout approach

Stand out the right way.

Coaches choose players who make the team better, are easy to coach, and never stop competing.

What coaches watch

Scanning before receiving. Direction of the first touch. Speed of decisions. Reaction when the ball is lost. Talk. How you respond to correction.

As an attacker

Coaches judge forwards on movement before the ball. Check toward the ball, then spin in behind. Make runs even when you don’t get the pass.

Take your first touch toward goal. Shoot when you see the frame. A shot on target beats a perfect pass that never comes.

Combine. A quick one two is worth more than beating three players alone.

Press from the front. When your team loses it, you are the first defender. That’s the five second win back.

Cue: “Move, face up, shoot.”

Before the first whistle

Shake the coach’s hand and look him in the eye. Be early to every line. Warm up like it already counts.

In the drills

Play simple and quick. Two touch and move. Talk every rep: “Man on,” “Time,” “Turn.” Quality over flash.

Lose it, win it back

When you lose the ball, react in the first five seconds. Press immediately. Coaches notice that more than any trick.

Take coaching visibly

When a coach corrects you, nod, say “got it,” and show it on the very next rep. That’s the player they want to coach all season.

In the scrimmage

Ask for the ball, move into space, make the safe pass when in doubt. Win your individual battles. A great recovery run can matter more than a goal.

After it ends

Thank the coaches by name and help pick up the cones. The last impression counts as much as the first.
